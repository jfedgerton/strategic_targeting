# Appendix text draft — history-chosen exclusions (for the PI to edit)

**Coalitions excluded on historical grounds.** A small number of coalition disputes carry much of the relational result, so we re-estimate the main model after removing coalition interventions that were justified as punishment for a violation of international norms. These exclusion lists were written after the influential disputes were known. They are retrospective robustness checks and not a preregistered test. We therefore report every list we built rather than a preferred one.

A dispute enters a list when it meets two conditions. First, the coalition's main public justification at the time was one of five triggers. These were aggression by the target against another state, mass atrocities, attacks on foreign nationals or diplomats, defiance of UN Security Council resolutions, or an attack by the target state or by forces it harboured. Restoring an elected government is not a trigger. Territorial, colonial, succession, debt-collection and alliance aims exclude a case when they were the main aim. Second, at least three states on the coalition side used force in the dispute, counted from the COW-MID participant data (highest action of 14 or more). Coalitions on both sides of a dispute were eligible. We screened every fatal dispute with three or more distinct initiators on the initiating side or three or more joiners on the target side.

Two rulings were added after an independent second coder reviewed the cases blind to the estimates and to our coding. Collective defense of an attacked state counts as a response to aggression only when the coalition framed it as enforcing a multilateral norm, as in the UN-backed coalitions in Korea and the Gulf. Assistance given under an alliance or at the attacked government's request counts as an alliance aim. The count of states using force follows the MID participant data even where case histories suggest fewer states fired. This affects the Nanking intervention of 1927, where the histories record British and American fire only.

Table A.X lists the screened disputes, the coding of each and the sources. We estimate four versions. The UN list drops Korea and the Gulf War. The strict list adds the Boxer intervention, the Nanking intervention, Navarino, the Iraq no-fly zones, Kosovo, Afghanistan and Grenada. A second strict list keeps the two borderline cases, Navarino and Grenada, in the sample. The expanded list adds Iraq 1997–2003, whose single MID contains a two-state operation and a separate 2003 coalition, ECOMOG in Sierra Leone, and Shaba I. An event is dropped when any of its source disputes is listed, so a listed dispute is removed whole.
